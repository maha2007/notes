Reading notes · Nature 532, 453–458 (2016)

Natural speech reveals the semantic maps that tile human cerebral cortex

Huth, de Heer, Griffiths, Theunissen & Gallant · UC Berkeley

MMaha Nawaz · October 13, 2026

The question: when you understand a word, where in the brain does its meaning light up, and does each spot care about a different kind of meaning? Earlier studies tested a handful of hand-picked categories. This paper lets people listen to real stories and maps meaning across the whole cortex at once.

The 60-second version
  1. 7 people lay in an fMRI scanner listening to 2+ hours of The Moth stories.
  2. Every word was turned into 985 numbers describing what words it usually appears near.
  3. For every tiny cube of brain (a voxel), a regression learned which of those word-features make it respond. These models predicted brain activity on a new story across a large "semantic system".
  4. PCA found ~4 dimensions of meaning shared across people. The biggest one: people & social vs perceptual & physical.
  5. A new generative model (PrAGMATiC) turned the noisy maps into an atlas of ~140 areas, spread across both hemispheres and strikingly similar across people.
The whole paper as a pipeline. Each stage feeds the next; the tags show which figure covers it.

Primer: what fMRI actually measures

fMRI doesn't see neurons fire. When a patch of brain works harder, fresh oxygenated blood rushes in a few seconds later. The scanner picks up that change in blood oxygen, called the BOLD signal.

The brain is chopped into a 3-D grid of voxels (3-D pixels). Here each voxel is 2.24 × 2.24 × 4.1 mm, about a grain of rice, and the scanner takes a full-brain snapshot every 2 seconds (the TR). So each person yields a big table: tens of thousands of voxels, each with a time series of ~3,700 values.

Two facts that shape everything below
1. It's slow. BOLD peaks ~5 s after the neural event, so the model must account for delay.
2. It's noisy. Even a perfect model can't predict every wiggle, so prediction scores of r ≈ 0.3–0.6 are considered strong.
Figure 1a · Voxel-wise model estimation

Turning stories into numbers a brain can be fit to

Figure 1a packs four ideas into one strip. Here they are pulled apart.

Step 1 · Each word becomes 985 numbers

The authors picked 985 common "basis" words (from Wikipedia's list of 1000 basic words). For every word in the stories, they counted how often it appears within 15 words of each basis word, across a huge text corpus (books, Wikipedia, Reddit, ~1.5 billion words). Counts were log-transformed and z-scored. The result is a 985-number fingerprint per word.

The intuition: words used in similar contexts mean similar things. "Month" and "week" both show up near "day", "year", "time", so their fingerprints match.

Illustrative fingerprints across 8 of the 985 basis words (values made up for the picture). The correlations on the right are the real ones reported in the paper: month vs week r = 0.74, month vs tall r = −0.22.

Step 2 · A story becomes 985 time series

Every word was time-stamped to the audio (via forced alignment). At the moment a word is spoken, its 985 numbers are dropped onto the timeline. Then the timeline is smoothed and resampled to match the scanner's 2-second snapshots. These are the wiggly "Semantic features" traces on the left of Fig 1a, labelled 'Above', 'Dance', 'Man', 'Seven' (four of the 985 basis words).

One feature channel (co-occurrence with the basis word "seven"). Numeric words like "four" push it up. Summed and resampled every 2 s, it becomes one of the 985 traces fed to the model.

Step 3 · Handling the brain's ~5-second delay

Because BOLD lags, a word heard now affects the signal 2–8 s later. Rather than assume a fixed delay shape, the model makes four copies of every feature, shifted by 2, 4, 6 and 8 s, and learns a separate weight for each. This is the FIR (finite impulse response) model. 985 semantic + 41 low-level features × 4 delays = 4,104 weights per voxel.

Why four delays. A word at t = 0 produces a slow blood-flow bump (curve). Four weights sample it at 2, 4, 6 and 8 s, so the model learns the bump's shape per voxel.

Step 4 · The regression: features × weights = BOLD

The matrix equation in Fig 1a is just linear regression, run separately for every voxel. The colored numbers (2.8, −0.4, 0.7…) are example weights: positive means "this feature makes this voxel respond more".

One voxel's model. With 4,104 weights and only ~3,700 time points, ordinary regression would overfit wildly, so they use ridge regression (a penalty that keeps weights small). They also include word rate, phoneme rate and 39 phoneme features so that low-level sound properties can't masquerade as meaning; those weights are thrown away afterwards.
Concrete example
A voxel in the angular gyrus might end up with big positive weights on features like co-occurrence with "mother", "friend", "family". Whenever the speaker talks about people, its predicted BOLD rises ~5 s later. That weight vector is the voxel's semantic tuning.

Testing the model on a story it has never heard

Reading a cortical flatmap

The cortex is a crumpled sheet about 2–4 mm thick. To see all of it at once, software inflates it and makes a few cuts, then presses it flat, like peeling an orange and flattening the peel on a table. The four small brains around Fig 1c show the same data on the folded surface; the big shapes are the flattened left (LH) and right (RH) hemispheres.

Figure 2 · The shared dimensions of meaning

Compressing 985 dimensions into 4

Each voxel now has 985 semantic weights. That's too many to look at directly. So the authors stacked the weight vectors of the best-predicted voxels (10,000 per person) and ran principal components analysis (PCA): find the directions along which voxels differ most.

PCA intuition. Each dot is one voxel's weight vector (2 of 985 dimensions shown). PC1 is the longest axis of the cloud: the single biggest way voxels disagree.
How many PCs are real? (Extended Data Fig 2, approximate.) Group PCs are compared with PCs of the stimulus itself. Only ~4 beat that baseline in most subjects.

The control is clever. Some structure in the weights could come from the stories themselves (e.g., if "people" words were simply most common). So they asked: does each brain-derived PC explain more variance than the corresponding PC of the stimulus features? Four dimensions passed in 6 of 7 subjects. Those 4 are the paper's "shared semantic space".

Figure 2a · What the dimensions mean

Placing 10,470 words in the space

PCs are just directions in a 985-D space, so to interpret them the authors projected every word from the stories onto them. Then k-means clustering grouped words into 12 categories, labelled by hand. Figure 2b–c · Painting the brain

From a voxel's weights to a colour on cortex

Fig 2b uses the same colour code on voxels. Take a voxel's 985 weights, project them onto PC1–3 to get three numbers, use those as R, G, B, and paint that spot. A green voxel responds more to visual and numeric words; a magenta one to social and mental words.

Toy mapping: each score from −1 to +1 sets one colour channel from 0 to 255; the nearest category is found by colour. The paper's real colour scale is more tuned, but the logic is the same.

What Fig 2b shows (subject S2, flatmap plus folded views): semantic tuning is not one blob per category. It forms intricate patchworks across prefrontal cortex, LTC and MTC, LPC and MPC. Black regions are voxels the model couldn't predict.

What Fig 2c shows: the same flatmaps for three other subjects. The patchworks look strikingly alike, for example social (red/pink) patches in the same spots of parietal cortex across people. That consistency is what motivates building a single group atlas.

Figure 3a · PrAGMATiC

A generative model of cortical maps

Problem: brains differ in size and folding, so you can't just overlay seven maps and average. PrAGMATiC (Probabilistic And Generative Model of Areas Tiling the Cortex) solves this by describing how a map is generated, with some parameters shared across people and some unique to each.

It has two halves, matching the two halves of Fig 3a:

Arrangement model · where areas sit
E(H; L) = Σ (dij − Lij)²
P(H; L) ∝ e−E

Each area is a single point (centroid). Neighbouring centroids are joined by springs. L = each spring's ideal length (shared). H = actual centroid positions (unique per person). Stretched or squished springs store energy; low-energy layouts are probable. Springs also tie areas to 19 known landmarks (✕) like auditory cortex or FFA, found separately in each person.

Emission model · what each area means
E(V | H; M) ∝ Σ (Mnearest − Vl)²
P(V | H; M) ∝ e−E

Every point on cortex belongs to its nearest centroid, which carves cortex into a Voronoi tessellation. Each area has a mean semantic value M (a 4-D point in PC space, shared). Each voxel's observed value V is that mean plus Gaussian noise.

Total probability = arrangement × map: P(V, H; M, L) = P(H; L) · P(V | H; M). Training adjusts L and M to make the seven real maps probable and made-up maps improbable (a contrastive-divergence-style maximum-likelihood rule). Because individual H is free, nobody's brain has to be warped to a template.

Try it: drag the area centroids (circles)
Spring energy E(H;L): 0
Drag a circle: springs turn orange when stretched, blue when squished, and energy rises (a less probable layout). Relax lets springs pull back. New subject keeps the same springs L and area meanings M but gives a different layout H, which is how one atlas fits seven different brains. ✕ = landmarks.
Figure 3b · Choosing the number of areas

How many areas tile the cortex?

The number of areas is a knob the model can't learn by itself. So: fit PrAGMATiC on 6 subjects, then use it to predict the 7th subject's BOLD responses from that person's anatomy and landmarks alone. Repeat for each held-out subject and each area count.

Approximate redraw of the mean curves. Performance climbs fast up to ~64 areas, then flattens. It stops improving significantly at 192 areas for the left hemisphere and 128 for the right. Coloured lines in the real figure are individual subjects S1–S7.

Why are the r values so small here (~0.06) compared with Fig 1c (up to 0.6)? Here the map is predicted for a brand-new person from a group atlas, averaged over all of cortex, including many voxels that aren't semantic at all. The point is the shape of the curve, not the height.

Reading the final map

The 192 + 128 areas include non-semantic ones (e.g., motor cortex). Each area was kept only if its semantic model predicted better than a low-level model (word rate, phoneme rate, phonemes). Patterns worth spotting in the atlas:

LPC & MPCA bullseye: central areas (angular gyrus, subparietal sulcus) are social; surrounding rings are numeric, visual, tactile.
SPFCMedial parts mostly social; dorsolateral parts mixed.
LTCFewer distinct areas than expected, likely because MRI signal is poor in the anterior temporal lobe (black hatching).
NetworksLPC, MPC and SPFC belong to the default mode network, the system active in introspection and daydreaming. The authors speculate the same map may represent conscious thought, not just heard speech.

Takeaways and caveats

What the paper showed

BroadMeaning isn't in one "language area". Semantic selectivity covers large parts of temporal, parietal and prefrontal cortex.
BilateralBoth hemispheres carry rich, fairly symmetric maps. This conflicts with lesion studies pointing to the left hemisphere. Possibly narrative stories engage the right hemisphere more than single words do.
SharedThe layout is similar across people. Could be innate wiring, or shared upbringing (all subjects were educated Westerners).
MethodData-driven: no hypothesis about categories was needed. Two hours of listening was enough to map the whole cortex.

Limitations to keep in mind

FeaturesWord co-occurrence is a bag-of-words model: "dog bites man" = "man bites dog". No syntax or context. Later work by the same lab swapped in contextual language-model embeddings and predicted much better.
AreasPrAGMATiC assumes each area is uniform inside. Some maps may really be smooth gradients.
Sample7 people, including two authors. Labels for PCs and categories were assigned by hand.
ColourRGB shows only 3 of 4 dimensions, and two voxels can look similar while differing on PC4.

Glossary

BOLD
Blood-oxygen signal fMRI measures; a slow proxy for neural activity.
Voxel
One 3-D pixel of brain, here 2.24 × 2.24 × 4.1 mm.
Encoding model
Predicts brain activity from stimulus features (as here). The reverse is a decoding model.
Ridge regression
Linear regression with a penalty on large weights, to avoid overfitting.
FIR
Separate weights per time delay, so the model learns the response's timing.
FDR
False discovery rate: multiple-comparisons correction across thousands of voxels.
Voronoi
Partition where every point belongs to its nearest centre.
