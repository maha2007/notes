Language Neurons, One by One

Cai, Kfir, Jamali, Huang, Kim, Cash & Williams · "Mapping the neuronal building blocks of human language with language models" · Massachusetts General Hospital / Harvard

MMaha Nawaz · October 13, 2026

The three papers I read before this one (Huth, Fedorenko, Frankland & Greene) watched language through fMRI: millimetre voxels averaging hundreds of thousands of neurons. This one records individual neurons in people having real conversations, and asks what single cells care about when we build a sentence.

This is a primary research article, not a review. It's the first in this set to measure single cells, and the first to use a large language model as the yardstick.

The 60-second version
  1. 8 epilepsy patients with tiny 96-electrode arrays in frontal and temporal cortex chatted naturally: 10,460 words, 579 neurons.
  2. Each spoken word was labelled with grammar features from a parser: part of speech, which phrase it's in, how deep in the sentence tree, its grammatical relation.
  3. About half the neurons were selective, each for a specific grammatical feature, with little overlap. Some track word-level grammar (nouns, verbs). Others track sentence structure (phrase type, tree depth, phrases closing).
  4. You can decode these features from the population, and the codes generalize to new sentences.
  5. An LLM's contextual embeddings predicted neural activity best, better than grammar or word meaning alone, with the signal peaking ~1 s before the word is spoken.
  6. Neurons are found in both hemispheres, but left-hemisphere neurons are more strongly tuned. Single neurons are far more selective than the field potentials around them.

Setup: how you record a neuron in a talking person

The patients were already having electrodes placed to locate the source of their seizures. With consent, the surgeons also inserted a Utah array into tissue that was planned for removal anyway: a 4 × 4 mm bed of 96 needle electrodes, each tipped finer than a hair. Each needle can pick up the electrical spikes of nearby individual neurons.

People8 adults (3 female, 5 male, age 27–52), normal language. One array each.
SitesLeft: 2 anterior temporal, 1 posterior temporal, 1 prefrontal. Right: 1 anterior temporal, 2 posterior temporal, 1 prefrontal. All in areas known to engage in language.
TaskFree conversation. Asked "Do you want a phone right now?", someone might answer "Yes, keep it there just in case, and then just bring that back over." Sentences are built fresh, not read or repeated. 1,895 sentences in total.
WindowNeural activity from 400 to 100 ms before each word is spoken: the planning period. So this is about producing language, not hearing it.

Figures 1d & 2b · Two ways to parse a sentence

What the grammar labels mean

The authors used two standard NLP parsers (from AllenNLP) to label every word automatically. They describe sentence structure in two different ways.

Constituency tree (Fig 1d): words nest inside phrases
S = sentence, NP = noun phrase, VP = verb phrase, ADVP = adverb phrase, PP = prepositional phrase. Each word's depth = levels between it and the top. "They": pronoun (PRP), in a noun phrase, depth 3, position 1, matching the paper's example.
Dependency tree (Fig 2b): words point to the word they modify
The verb "came" is the root. Every other word hangs off a "head" word with a labelled relation: nsubj = nominal subject, advmod = adverbial modifier, prep = preposition, pobj = object of preposition, det = determiner. "They": nominal subject, dependency depth 2.

Figure 2 · Selectivity and decoding

Specialist neurons for specific grammar

A neuron is "selective" for a feature if its firing before words of one category (e.g., adverbs) is significantly higher than before all other words (Mann–Whitney test, corrected for multiple comparisons).

Fig 2a (left) + 2c (left). % of the 579 neurons selective for each feature. Each is well above chance, and held up when random subsets of words were dropped and when word frequency, length and surprisal were controlled.

Do the same neurons do everything? No.

The heatmaps in Fig 2a/2c are coincidence matrices: the diagonal is the % of neurons selective for that feature, off-diagonal cells are the % selective for both. Dark diagonal + pale off-diagonal = specialists. Across all feature pairs, only 2.2% of neurons overlapped. Pitch-tuned neurons (12%) almost never overlapped with grammar neurons (≤2.2%), so the grammar effects aren't just voice changes.

Fig 2c (bottom right), neuron counts. Left: even though constituency depth, dependency depth and word position all rise through a sentence, mostly different neurons track each (72, 48, 39 alone; only 2 track all three). Right: same for phrase type, part of speech and dependency relation.

Fig 2d: can you read grammar off the neurons?

Train a classifier on 70% of words (neural activity → label), test on the other 30%. Repeat 1,000 times. The histograms show the accuracy distribution; the triangle is the mean; the dotted line is chance.

Fig 2d, mean single-word decoding accuracy vs chance. E.g., constituency depth: 31.5% correct where guessing gives 14.3%. Modest numbers, because one word's few spikes are noisy; averaging over several words raises accuracy (Extended Data Fig 2).

Fig 2e is a UMAP: each dot is a neuron, positioned by its tuning profile and coloured by its preferred feature. Neurons with the same preference cluster together, so feature types form distinct groups in "tuning space".

Figure 3 · Language models as a yardstick

Do neurons encode words in context?

"Dog" is a noun in both "The dog ate the bone" and "The man walked the dog", but it's the subject in one and the object in the other. And "bark" means different things in "The dog has a loud bark" and "The tree has thick bark". So the question is: do neurons encode feature combinations and context?

The method is the encoding-model logic from Huth 2016: turn each word into a vector, then fit a ridge regression from vector to firing rate on all sentences but one, and predict the left-out sentence. Predictivity = correlation between predicted and actual firing.

Fig 3a, redrawn. Four ways to describe a word, all reduced to 30 dimensions with PCA (so bigger models don't win just by having more numbers), then mapped to neural activity.
Fig 3b. Population predictivity. Contextual LLM embeddings win (~0.05) over syntax + meaning combined (~0.015). Small r, but significantly above chance and above the others.
Fig 3c. Give the LLM more preceding words and it predicts neurons better, plateauing at ~5 words. Neurons carry a few words of context.
3d controlsShuffle which sentence goes with which neural data → predictivity ≈ 0. Feed the LLM random tokens instead of the real words → ≈ 0. So the fit depends on the actual words in the actual sentence.
Only 1.8%of neurons encoded both syntactic and semantic information. Individual cells lean one way; the population carries the combination.
3eHeatmap: LLM layer (y) × time relative to word onset (x). Bright = high predictivity. The bright band sits before 0, peaking ~1 s before the word is spoken, in middle layers. The brain is encoding the word's contextual meaning while planning it.
3fEach dot = one feature's neurons, placed at their average peak time and LLM layer. Different feature-tuned groups peak at different layers and times, from about 1.4 s to 0.4 s before the word.
Schematic of Fig 3e. Brightest region ≈ middle layers, ~1 s before the word. Vicuna-7B has 32 layers; early layers are closer to raw tokens, middle layers carry the richest contextual meaning.

Figure 5 · Neurons vs local field potentials

Zoom in and the code gets sharper

The same electrode records two things. Spikes are the outputs of one or a few neurons right at the tip. The local field potential (LFP) is the slow, summed electrical activity of the surrounding population, closer to what ECoG (and loosely fMRI) see.

Key numbers from Fig 5. At the very same sites, single neurons were selective far more often and far more strongly than the LFP. Only 19% of sites had neurons and LFP tuned to the same feature.

Left panel sorts all 579 channels by neuron z-score (orange, rising to the right) and plots the LFP z-score at the same channel (blue) underneath. The orange climbs to 5–10; the blue stays near zero. Middle: neuron vs LFP z-scores barely correlate (r = 0.21). Right: the LFP coincidence matrix shows only a faint diagonal.

Analogy
A stadium crowd's roar (LFP) tells you a goal happened. Individual fans' chants (neurons) tell you which team they support. Average them and the detail cancels out. This is why fMRI studies can find "language areas" but struggle to separate syntax from meaning inside them.

How it fits with the other three papers

Huth 2016Cited (ref. 9). Same encoding-model recipe (features → ridge regression → predict held-out data → correlation), but with neurons instead of voxels, and production instead of listening. It also delivers the upgrade I flagged in my Huth notes: contextual LLM embeddings beat word-level (word2vec, like Huth's co-occurrence) features.
Fedorenko 2024Cited (ref. 28). Arrays sat in regions that respond to her language localizers (from group maps, not each patient's own scan). Her review argued no language area specializes in syntax vs meaning. This paper finds specialization at the cell level: syntax- and meaning-tuned neurons interleaved in the same patch. That matches her prediction of "heterogeneity among interleaved neural populations". Both agree on left-hemisphere dominance, but here right-hemisphere neurons are present, just more weakly tuned.
Frankland & Greene 2020Their language of thought needs reusable parts plus role binding. Here there are neurons for grammatical relations (nominal subject, object of preposition) that generalize across sentences, a cellular version of role slots. One difference: they found lmSTC tracked event roles (agent/patient) rather than grammatical subject/object. This paper labels grammatical dependencies and doesn't test event roles. The population-combines-what-single-cells-separate result echoes their additive vs multiplicative binding question.

Caveats

Patients8 people with epilepsy; tissue near seizure foci may not be typical.
CoverageOne 4 × 4 mm array per person, 8 sites total. Classic areas like left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca's region) weren't sampled; the authors flag this.
Effect sizesSelectivity is statistical: ~10–16% of neurons per feature, single-word decoding ~2× chance, LLM predictivity r ≈ 0.05. Real but small.
LabelsGrammar categories come from automatic parsers, which can disagree; features overlap (a noun is usually in a noun phrase), though the authors control for this.
Production onlyIt's unknown whether the same neurons encode heard language.
LocalizersSites weren't defined with each patient's own fMRI language localizer, the standard Fedorenko argues for, so some neurons may belong to neighbouring speech-planning or motor areas.

Glossary

AP / spike
Action potential: one neuron's electrical output pulse.
Spike sorting
Assigning spikes to individual neurons by their waveform shape.
Utah array
10 × 10 grid of needle microelectrodes over 4 × 4 mm.
LFP
Local field potential: summed slow activity of the local population.
PSTH / raster
Average firing rate over time / spike dots per trial.
Constituency parse
Tree of nested phrases.
Dependency parse
Tree of head → dependent word relations.
Predictivity
Correlation between model-predicted and actual firing.
z-score (modulation)
How far the preferred-feature firing rate is above other words, in standard deviations.
UMAP
Method to draw high-dimensional data in 2-D, keeping similar points close.
