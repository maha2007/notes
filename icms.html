Neural mechanisms of intracortical microstimulation, explained figure by figure

Hughes, Chen, Grill & Kozai · Univ. of Pittsburgh / Duke · vol. 10, pp. 197–213 · doi:10.1038/s41551-025-01583-6

MMaha Nawaz · October 14, 2026

This review asks one question: when you push a tiny electric current into the brain through an implanted needle, which neurons fire, why those, and what does that mean for the sensation the person feels? The technique is called ICMS, short for intracortical microstimulation. It is the "write" half of a brain–computer interface. Decoding speech from brain signals is "reading"; ICMS is how you send touch or vision back in.

Before the figures

Five things you need to know about neurons

1. A neuron is a leaky battery with a trigger. Inside, it sits at about −70 mV relative to outside. Push it up to roughly −55 mV and it fires an action potential: an all-or-nothing voltage spike that travels down its output cable. Below threshold, nothing travels. That makes it a comparator with a threshold, much like a digital gate.

2. It has three parts. Dendrites are the inputs. The soma (cell body) adds the inputs up. The axon is the output wire, often long and branching to reach other neurons far away. Where one neuron's axon meets the next neuron's dendrite is a synapse.

3. The trigger is made of sodium channels. Tiny voltage-controlled gates (voltage-gated Na⁺ channels) open when the membrane voltage rises. Sodium ions rush in, the voltage rises further, more gates open, and the spike runs away. Axons are packed with these gates, which is why axons are the easiest part to trigger. Hold onto that fact. It explains most of this paper.

Deep dive · a question I had

"How does the sodium trigger work in more detail?"

The battery: why the inside sits at −70 mV

Think of the membrane as a charged capacitor with two kinds of voltage-controlled switches in it. Pumps in the membrane constantly move ions to build two concentration gradients: lots of Na⁺ outside, lots of K⁺ inside. At rest a few K⁺ leak channels are open. K⁺ trickles out down its gradient and carries positive charge with it, which leaves the inside at about −70 mV.

The rest of this deep dive continues below: the channel's four states, the feedback loop, the spike, and why axons are the easiest target.

Inside the sodium channel

Each channel is a protein tunnel through the membrane with two gates in series, and both must be open for Na⁺ to pass. The m-gate (activation) sits on the outer end and is pulled open by a charged voltage sensor (the S4 segment) that slides up when the inside becomes more positive. The h-gate (inactivation) is a "ball on a chain" on the inside that swings in and plugs the tunnel about a millisecond later. One channel cycles through four states during every spike:

  • 1
    Resting (−70 mV). The inside is negative, which attracts the sensor's + charges down. The sensor holds the m-gate shut. The h-gate hangs free, so the channel is closed but ready.
  • 2
    Activated (rising past −55 mV). Something makes the inside less negative: a synaptic input, or an electrode pulse. The sensor's + charges are no longer held down, so it slides up and yanks the m-gate open in about 0.1 ms. Na⁺ pours in down its gradient, which makes the inside more positive, which opens more channels nearby.
  • 3
    Inactivated (peak, about +40 mV). The h-gate is slower. About 1 ms in, the ball swings into the inner mouth and plugs it, even though the m-gate is still open. Na⁺ inflow stops. Meanwhile slower K⁺ channels open and let K⁺ out, pulling the voltage back down.
  • 4
    Recovering (undershoot, about −80 mV). Back at negative voltage, the sensor drops and closes the m-gate. The ball takes a millisecond or two to un-plug. Until it does, the channel cannot open again, however hard you push. This is the refractory period, and it caps how fast a neuron can fire.

Where the electrode comes in. A cathodic pulse makes the fluid just outside the membrane more negative. Membrane voltage is inside minus outside, so the inside looks relatively more positive. Sensors near the tip feel exactly what they would feel in step 2. Axons have the most sensors per µm of membrane and the smallest "capacitor" to charge, so they reach threshold first.

Why axons are the easiest target

  • 1
    Channel density. The axon's starting segment and the nodes of Ranvier (gaps in the axon's fatty myelin insulation) pack roughly 10–50× more Na⁺ channels per µm² than the cell body. More switches per patch means a smaller nudge starts the loop.
  • 2
    Small capacitor. The soma is a big blob of membrane, a large capacitor that takes more charge to move by the same voltage. A thin axon segment is a small capacitor and charges quickly. That also gives axons short chronaxies (about 100–300 µs), which suits clinical ~200 µs pulses.
  • 3
    Long, thin geometry. A long cable passing the tip sees the outside voltage change along its length, and that change is what pushes the membrane (the activating function, explained under Figure 1). A compact round cell body doesn't get this effect.
  • 4
    Myelin focuses it. On insulated axons, all the action is squeezed into the nodes, so a node near the tip is a very sensitive trigger point.

The consequence runs through the whole paper. The spike starts in whichever axon passes near the tip, not in the cell body that axon belongs to, and that body may be far away. That is why activated cells are scattered (Fig 2), why ICMS skips the cell's normal input processing (Fig 1g), and why spikes travel backward as well as forward (antidromic).

4. Signals normally flow one way: dendrites → soma → axon → next neuron. That is called orthodromic ("correct direction"). A spike started mid-axon also travels backwards toward the soma. That is antidromic.

5. The cortex is a 2–3 mm thick sheet with 6 layers. Sensory input from the thalamus (the brain's relay station) lands mainly in layer 4, goes up to layers 2/3, then down to layer 5/6 outputs. The sheet is also split side-to-side into columns about 300–600 µm wide. Each column handles one patch of skin or one spot in the visual field.

Basic mechanisms: how a current pulse makes a neuron fire

Figure 1 is the foundation. It starts at the patient and zooms down to ion channels, then shows how pulse settings and distance decide who fires.

  • a
    The setup. A Utah array (a 4×4 mm bed of about 100 silicon needles, each 1–1.5 mm long) is implanted in sensory cortex. A cable runs to a stimulator. Since 2015, people with spinal-cord injury have felt touch on their hand this way, and a blind participant has seen dots of light (phosphenes) from an array in visual cortex.
  • b
    Zooming in on one electrode tip. Current from the tip pushes charged ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) around in the fluid. This shifts the voltage across nearby cell membranes. The shift flips open the voltage sensor of sodium channels; ions flow in, and if enough add up, the neuron spikes. Small sub-threshold nudges can add up over space (many dendrites) and time. How long a nudge lingers is set by the membrane time constant, about 10 ms on average (range 2–140 ms).
  • c
    The pulse itself. Two knobs: amplitude (how much current, in microamps, µA) and pulse width (how long, in microseconds, µs). Amplitude × width = charge (nanocoulombs, nC). Clinical systems use biphasic, charge-balanced pulses: a negative (cathodic) phase, a short gap (interphase interval, IPI), then a positive (anodic) phase that pulls the same charge back out. Single-direction (monophasic) pulses leave net charge behind, which corrodes the electrode and damages tissue.
  • d
    The activating function. A rule for where along an axon the pulse actually depolarizes (excites) the membrane. A cathodic pulse depolarizes the axon right under the tip. An anodic pulse hyperpolarizes (quiets) under the tip but creates two excited spots off to the sides, called virtual cathodes. So anodic pulses can still fire neurons, but need more current. Whether virtual cathodes matter for tiny brain electrodes is still unproven.
  • e
    The strength–duration curve. Short pulses need more current; long pulses need less. The curve flattens at the rheobase (the minimum current that works even with an infinitely long pulse). The chronaxie is the pulse width at which you need exactly 2× rheobase. It is a single number that says how "fast" a neural element is. In rat cortex chronaxie was 200–400 µs, which is right where clinical pulse widths sit.
  • f
    Threshold vs distance. The farther a neuron is from the tip, the more current it takes to fire it. The curve is a bowl. Direct activation (the pulse hits the axon) has a steep bowl. Synaptic activation (a directly-hit neuron then excites its neighbor) needs more current near the tip but spreads farther.
  • g
    Direct vs indirect activation. Direct: the pulse starts a spike in an axon. It is fast (under 5 ms) but bypasses all the normal input math the soma would do, so it is "non-physiological". Indirect: neurons fire because a directly-hit neuron excited them through a synapse. It is slower but runs through normal circuitry, so it should feel more natural.
Deep dive · a question I had

"What is the activating function? How does depolarizing equal getting excited? Why would a cathodic pulse excite the axon, an anodic pulse quiet it, and why does it create two excited spots off to the sides?"

The activating function, step by step

1 · "Depolarize" means "move toward the trigger"

At rest the inside of the axon sits at about −70 mV. The spike fires at about −55 mV. Depolarizing is moving up this scale. Push up 15 mV or more and you cross threshold, the sodium gates open, and the spike fires. That is all "excite" means. Hyperpolarizing moves the voltage down, further from threshold, so the neuron gets harder to fire. That is all "quiet" means.

2 · What "inside", "outside" and "off to the sides" mean

I also wondered: what does "off to the sides" mean, and why would "the inside settle near −8"?

Picture the axon as a garden hose lying flat, with the electrode tip hovering just above one spot on it. Inside is the fluid core within the axon. Outside is the brain fluid around it. Off to the sides means points further along the same axon, away from the spot directly beneath the tip. The axon is long, so different points along it sit at different distances from the electrode.

3 · The key fact: membrane voltage is inside minus outside

Vm = Vinside − Voutside

The electrode never touches the inside. It only changes the outside fluid. The inside of the axon, though, is a long, salty, conductive core, so it behaves like a wire. A wire can't hold a sharp voltage bump at one spot, because charge flows along it and evens it out, like water levelling in a pipe. So the inside settles near the average of what the outside is doing along that stretch.

The rule. Wherever the outside is below that average, Vm goes up (depolarized, excitable). Wherever the outside is above it, Vm goes down (hyperpolarized, quieted).

All numbers below are changes caused by the pulse, added on top of the resting −70 mV. What matters is how the pulse shifts the difference between inside and outside.

4 · Cathodic pulse: the spot under the tip gets excited

A cathode is the negative electrode, so it makes the outside fluid negative, most strongly right at the tip.

At the sides the outside barely moved (2) but the inside dropped 8, so the membrane goes down to −76.

6 · Why anodic pulses need more current

The side spots are much weaker than the centre. In the table, +6 mV at the sides doesn't reach the +15 mV needed to fire. For a point electrode, the side peaks are only about 20% as strong as the centre peak, so in theory you need roughly 5× more anodic current to fire an axon through a virtual cathode than with a cathodic pulse right under the tip.

7 · So what is the "activating function"?

It is the name for this "how far is this spot from the average of its neighbours" calculation, done at every point along the axon. Mathematically it is the curvature (second derivative) of the outside voltage along the axon. For a discrete cable:

f(here) = V(left) + V(right) − 2·V(here)

Positive values mark spots that will depolarize; negative values mark spots that will hyperpolarize. The blue and red curves in the computed Fig 1d above are exactly this.

8 · Why it's "unproven" for tiny brain electrodes

Virtual cathodes have been clearly measured in heart tissue, but with large electrodes and long pulses. With brain microelectrodes and short pulses of about 200 µs, the side peaks are weak, and it isn't yet clear whether they're ever strong enough to fire neurons in practice.

"What on earth is a chronaxie?"

Chronaxie: how fast a neuron responds to a push

It comes from a simple experiment. Take one neuron and one electrode. For each pulse width, find the smallest current that makes it fire:

Pulse widthSmallest current that fires it
50 µs70 µA
100 µs40 µA
300 µs ← chronaxie20 µA (2 × rheobase)
1,000 µs13 µA
10,000 µs10.3 µA
infinitely long10 µA ← rheobase

Plot those pairs and you get the computed Fig 1e curve above. The floor (10 µA) is the rheobase: the weakest current that can ever fire the neuron, however long you hold it. The chronaxie is the pulse width where you need exactly 2× the rheobase. Here 2 × 10 = 20 µA happens at 300 µs, so the chronaxie is 300 µs.

  • S
    Short pulse: you must pour hard to reach the line before time runs out. High current, but the leak barely matters.
  • L
    Long pulse: you can pour gently, but the leak keeps stealing water, so you still need some minimum flow. That minimum is the rheobase.
  • C
    Chronaxie is roughly how long the bucket takes to fill noticeably. Fast elements (small, leaky bucket) have short chronaxies; slow ones (big bucket) have long ones.

Why anyone cares

  • 1
    It's a fingerprint for what you're hitting. Axons have short chronaxies, about 100–300 µs. Cell bodies and dendrites are slower, often 1 ms or more. In the paper, rat cortex gave 200–400 µs, consistent with ICMS firing axons.
  • 2
    It's the efficient pulse width. In this simple model, the energy needed to fire is lowest when the pulse width equals the chronaxie. Clinical ICMS uses 200–400 µs pulses, right around it.
  • 3
    Pulse width could pick targets. Since elements differ in chronaxie, short versus long pulses could in principle favour axons versus cell bodies, one of the pulse-shape ideas the paper calls underexplored.

The name is Greek chronos (time) + axia (value): "the time value". It was coined in 1909.

Think of a crowd and a megaphone. Direct activation is tapping someone's shoulder. Indirect activation is that person turning around and telling their friends. Tapping is fast and certain, but it skips the conversation that normally decides whether someone speaks up.
Takeaway. ICMS mostly fires axons, not cell bodies. Cathode-first pulses work at lower currents. More current or longer pulses recruit more neurons, but with diminishing returns. And every directly-hit neuron also excites others downstream, so one pulse sets off a small network event.
Figure 2

Turning up the current: where do activated neurons appear?

Two classic findings seemed to contradict each other.

Stoney, 1968: activation follows a tidy distance rule. The current needed to fire a neuron grows with the square of its distance:

i = k · r²   →   r = √(i / k),   k ≈ 1,292 µA/mm²

Example: at 15 µA, r = √(15/1292) mm ≈ 0.108 mm, so everything within about 108 µm fires. To double the radius you need 4× the current.

Histed, 2009, using two-photon microscopy to watch cells light up: activated cell bodies are sparse and scattered up to about a millimetre away, even at low current, and the spread barely changes with amplitude.

Figure 2 is a computer model (Kumaravelu et al., 2022) that shows both are true at once. The current activates axons in a compact sphere around the tip. But those axons belong to cell bodies that may sit far away. So if you look at axons, you see Stoney's sphere. If you look at cell bodies, you see Histed's sparse cloud.

  • a
    3D column of cortex coloured by layer (L1 pink, L2/3 orange, L4 green, L5 blue, L6 grey). Red dots are activated axons. They fill a black dashed sphere around the tip that grows from 5 to 50 µA.
  • b
    Same simulation, but red now marks activated cell bodies. They are spread through the whole column at every amplitude. Only the density rises.
  • c
    Top-down polar plots of the axon sites. At 5 µA they all sit within 100 µm; at 15 µA they reach 200–300 µm.
  • d
    Same view for cell bodies. Already at 5 µA they reach about 1,000 µm, and they barely spread further at 50 µA. They just fill in.
Deep dive · a question I had

"Do the cell bodies activate or not? Is it sparse because only the axon activates?"

Yes, the red cell bodies fire, but the current never reaches them

The current hits their axon near the electrode. The spike starts there and travels backward along the axon to the cell body (antidromic), as well as forward to the axon's targets. So the cell body fires, even though it sits far outside the zone the current reaches.

  • A
    A, B and C fire. Each axon happens to cross the dashed zone. A spike starts there (✱) and runs back to the cell body, which may be hundreds of µm away.
  • D
    D stays silent. Its cell body is no farther away than A's, but its axon never passes through the zone.

Reading the slider with this in mind

Left panel: each red dot is a spot where a spike starts, all on axons inside the dashed ring. Right panel: each red dot is the cell body that axon belongs to. It's the same set of neurons, which is why the two counts always match: each left dot has a partner on the right. Grey dots are neurons whose axons didn't cross the ring.

Why sparse and spread out: axons are long and run in all directions, so the cells whose axons cross that tiny zone can live anywhere. Their bodies end up scattered up to about 600 µm away, mixed among grey neighbours that didn't fire. Raise the current and the ring grows, so more axons cross it and more red dots appear. They don't spread much further out, because they were already as far as axons reach. The pattern gets denser, not wider.

The electrode is a junction box in one room that hundreds of phone lines pass through. Zap the box and phones ring in houses all over the neighbourhood. Only the houses whose line runs through that box ring, wherever those houses are.

One simplification in the toy model: a cell body sitting right next to the electrode would also get hit directly. The toy only models the axon route, because that route is the paper's point.

What people report

In human studies, turning up amplitude makes a sensation feel stronger (intensity rose linearly, about 0.025 normalized units per µA for 1-second trains). It mostly does not make the touched area on the hand bigger. That fits the model: more current means denser activation (stronger), not a wider footprint (bigger). One recent study did see the projected field grow, so this point is not settled.

Takeaway. More current means more neurons firing in the same region, not a much larger region. Brightness goes up; the spot does not get much bigger.

Pulse shape and frequency

Pulse shape

Clinical groups use different recipes. Pittsburgh uses 200 µs cathodic, 100 µs gap, 400 µs anodic at half amplitude. Others use a symmetric 200 µs / 200 µs pulse. Shape matters because different parts of a neuron charge at different speeds. In rodents, a long, low cathodic lead (5–8× longer than the trailing phase) gave more focal activation around the electrode than an anodic lead. Models suggest cathode-first favours passing axons and anode-first favours nearby cell bodies. Pulse shape is a largely unexplored knob in humans.

Frequency (pulses per second)

Frequency changes the quality of a sensation: "touch" and "tingle" at some rates, "buzzing" and "tapping" at others. On a given electrode, the same settings give the same quality day after day.

Higher frequency helps detection up to about 100–200 Hz, then stops helping. The reason is simple arithmetic. At 100 Hz the gap between pulses is 10 ms, about the membrane time constant. Below that gap, leftover voltage from one pulse is still there when the next arrives and the two add up. Going faster than ~100–200 Hz adds little extra summation.

High frequency has a cost: neurons far from the electrode get suppressed, perhaps because they run out of energy or because inhibition builds up. Even 130 Hz and 200 Hz give distinguishable activity patterns.

Newer ideas move away from a steady tick. Varying the timing between pulses (temporal patterning), bursts, or randomized (stochastic) timing look more like how real neurons fire. Bursts kept sensations alive longer than continuous trains in humans.

Too much stimulation: permanent damage vs temporary fatigue

The top half of Figure 3 covers settings that kill tissue. The bottom half covers settings that are "safe" yet still make neurons temporarily less responsive.

  • a
    Shannon's safety model. Two quantities matter together: charge per phase (how much charge each pulse pushes) and charge density (that charge divided by electrode surface area; a tiny tip concentrates it). Plot one against the other: below a diagonal line is safe, above it is damage. Other knobs (frequency, duration) could be plotted the same way.
  • b
    What damage looks like. With non-damaging ICMS, labelled neurons around the electrode survive. With damaging ICMS, there is a hole of dead neurons and damaged tissue near the tip. Animal data: more than 4 nC per phase, frequencies above 50 Hz, and durations over 8 hours all raised damage. Halving charge (4 → 2 nC), halving density (200 → 100 µC/cm²), or duty-cycling (1 s on, 1 s off) improved survival.
  • c
    SIDNE builds up. Stimulation-Induced Depression of Neuronal Excitability. During 8 hours of continuous, non-damaging stimulation, the evoked response shrinks hour by hour. Average responses fell 0–80% and thresholds rose 0–5×.
  • d
    SIDNE recovers slowly. After 7 hours of ICMS, full recovery took 2 to 17 days. That is far slower than normal sensory adaptation (seconds to minutes), so it is a different mechanism. Candidates: potassium piling up outside cells, or toxic by-products of chemistry at the electrode surface.
  • e
    Depression during stimulation, seen with calcium imaging (neurons glow when active). At low frequency, neurons near the electrode stay steady or even ramp up (facilitation). At high frequency and amplitude, neurons far from the electrode fade quickly and stay quiet briefly after stimulation stops.
  • f
    Same story in spike recordings. Each row is a trial; each tick is a spike. Low settings: firing locks to each pulse, then a short quiet period afterwards. High settings: strong firing that fades over time, sometimes a rebound burst after stimulation stops, then quiet.

There are two speeds of fatigue. Rapid depression happens in the first few seconds and depends on stimulation settings. Slow depression builds over many seconds and depends on how strongly neurons were first activated. In people, a continuous ICMS sensation can fade to imperceptible in under a minute, which is a practical problem for a prosthetic hand you want to feel all day.

Takeaway. Stay under the damage line, and you still have to deal with fatigue. Strong, fast, continuous stimulation tires neurons on timescales from seconds to weeks.

Cortex wiring, and how an electrode disrupts it

The busiest figure. Its message is that natural sensation is an ordered event in a structured circuit, and ICMS hits that circuit all at once, in the wrong order, through an electrode that has damaged it.

  • a
    Folds limit reach. The cortex is crumpled into ridges (gyri) and grooves (sulci). Touch processing starts in area 3b, which lies inside the central sulcus, where a flat Utah array cannot reach (the ✗). Arrays go on area 1 on the crown of the ridge (the ✓). The inset shows a second problem: the cortex surface is curved but the array is rigid and its needles all have the same length. On tilted cortex, neighbouring needles end in different layers.
  • b
    Maps are folded too. Visual cortex V1 is laid out like a map of what you see (numbered patches 1–12 match regions of the visual field). Central vision sits near the surface. Peripheral vision lies deep in the calcarine fissure, out of reach.
  • c
    Columns, layers, and electrode types. A rigid single-site needle stimulates only at its tip, so one layer. A rigid multisite shank or a flexible polymer one has contacts along its length and can reach several layers.
  • d
    Natural activation, in order: thalamus → layer 4 → layer 2/3 → deeper layers. Along the way, inhibitory cells silence neighbouring columns (lateral inhibition), which sharpens the signal, like edge-enhancement in an image.
  • e
    ICMS activation. The shank has cut through local connections. Everything near the tip fires at once, regardless of its role. ICMS in layer 4 can catch axons passing up to 2/3 and down to 5. ICMS in layer 2/3 skips the layer-4 step entirely.
  • f
    The body's reaction to the implant: bleeding, nearby neurons dying, and glial encapsulation (support cells wall the electrode off like a scar). All of this pushes healthy neurons farther away and raises thresholds.
  • g
    Cell types fire in a sequence. Excitatory pyramidal cells (Pyr) do the main signalling. Inhibitory cells, only 10–20% of neurons, come in subtypes. PV cells are fast (up to 450 Hz) and give feedforward inhibition; they fire first. SOM cells are slower (under 10 Hz) and give feedback inhibition; they fire last. VIP cells inhibit other inhibitors. Normal touch recruits PV → Pyr → SOM.
  • h
    ICMS breaks the sequence. Near the electrode, Pyr, PV and SOM all fire at the same instant. For neurons recruited indirectly, the order is unknown (the "???" in the figure). The electrode also touches blood vessels and non-neuronal cells.

Beyond neurons

Electrical stimulation also activates glia (support cells) and touches blood vessels. Neurons signal to vessels, so ICMS can change local blood flow. ICMS increases deoxygenated hemoglobin nearby, and recovery from depression depends on oxygen coming back. That links back to Figure 3: fatigue may partly be an energy-supply problem. The authors suggest this could become a tool: steer blood flow to keep tissue healthy and electrodes working longer.

They also compare ICMS with optogenetics (making genetically chosen neurons fire with light). Optogenetics activates cell bodies of a chosen type; ICMS activates axons near the tip, whatever their type. Optogenetic patterns look more like natural sensation, but it needs genetic modification, so it is not a near-term option in humans.

Takeaway. Real perception is a timed sequence across layers, columns and cell types. ICMS fires a local blob simultaneously, in whatever layer the needle tip happens to land, inside tissue the implant has damaged. That gap is probably why ICMS sensations often feel "artificial".
Figure 5

Emerging approaches: better hardware, smarter pulses

  • a
    Fully implanted, wireless systems. Today's systems use a percutaneous pedestal, a connector through the skull and skin. It is an infection risk and a mechanical weak point: in non-human primates with rigid arrays, 48% of devices failed from acute mechanical issues like broken wires. Wireless implants controlled from a small portable device would fix this, but their long-term reliability is not yet proven.
  • b
    Small, distributed, flexible electrodes spread across all layers. Smaller and softer means less scarring and finer targeting. The trade-off: a smaller contact concentrates charge (higher density, see Fig. 3a), increases cross-talk between channels, and adds new ways to fail.
  • c
    Biomimetic pulse trains. Record what real neurons do during a touch: a big burst at onset, a lower steady rate, and a burst at offset. A model turns that into a matching ICMS amplitude or frequency profile. In people, this made sensations feel more natural, improved discrimination, and helped a robotic arm transfer objects faster. It also uses less total charge.
  • d
    Multielectrode modulation. Give each electrode its own pulse shape and timing to target different cell types. Overlapping fields from two electrodes can create a "virtual electrode" between them (current steering). Cycling through electrodes lets each rest, which fights the fatigue in Fig. 3.
Deep dive · a question I had

The review says: "Researchers have begun to build more sophisticated approaches to parameter modulation that more closely resemble physiological cortical activation (that is, are biomimetic) and use multiple electrodes. What does this mean?"

Copying biology in time, and in space

Researchers are improving ICMS in two ways: changing the stimulation over time so it looks like real brain activity, and spreading it across several electrodes instead of one.

The old way

Pick one electrode and one setting, say 60 µA at 100 Hz, and keep it running the whole time the robotic hand touches an object. That is the flat dashed line in the 5c figure above.

Biomimetic = "copying biology" in time

Real touch neurons don't fire steadily. They burst when contact starts, settle to a low rate while you hold, and burst again when you let go (the 5c figure). "Parameter modulation" just means changing the amplitude or frequency moment by moment to follow that shape. Example: you pick up a cup. A biomimetic pattern gives a strong jolt at contact ("I've got it"), a gentle background while you hold it, and another jolt when you set it down. People said this felt more natural, could tell intensities apart better, and moved objects faster with a robotic arm. It also uses less total charge, which is easier on the tissue.

Multiple electrodes = copying biology in space

Real touch activates many neurons spread across a patch of cortex, not one dense blob around a single tip. Using several electrodes together lets researchers:

  • 1
    Spread activation out so it looks more like natural input.
  • 2
    Steer it: split current between two electrodes (50/50, 80/20) and the activated spot moves somewhere between them, creating "virtual" electrode positions (the current-steering plot above).
  • 3
    Take turns: cycling through electrodes lets each patch of neurons rest, so the sensation fades more slowly (the fatigue problem in Fig 3).

The authors also push biophysical models, like the Blue Brain-based column model behind Figure 2, as a cheap test bench for new stimulation patterns before trying them in people. The missing piece is a model that goes from "which neurons fired" to "what the person felt".

Bottom line. ICMS works well enough to restore useful touch and crude vision today. Making it feel natural needs three things: hitting the right layer and cell types, firing them in the right order and rhythm, and doing it with electrodes the brain tolerates for years. Combining human reports (what it feels like) with animal experiments (what the neurons did) is the path forward.

Glossary

Action potential (spike)
The all-or-nothing voltage pulse a neuron fires once its membrane crosses threshold.
Depolarize / hyperpolarize
Push the membrane voltage toward firing / away from firing.
Cathodic / anodic
Negative / positive current phase at the electrode. Cathodic excites more easily.
Charge per phase; charge density
Current × pulse width (nC); that charge divided by the electrode area (µC/cm²). Both set the damage limit.
Rheobase; chronaxie
The lowest current that ever works; the pulse width at which you need twice that.
Orthodromic / antidromic
A spike travelling the normal direction / backwards toward the cell body.
Phosphene
A dot of light someone "sees" from stimulation of visual cortex.
Projected field
The patch of body (or visual field) where a stimulated electrode's sensation is felt.
SIDNE
Stimulation-induced depression of neuronal excitability. Reversible fatigue that lasts days.
Glia / gliosis
Non-neuronal support cells / their scar-like response that walls off an implant.
PV, SOM, VIP
Three families of inhibitory interneurons named after marker proteins. Fast feedforward, slow feedback, and inhibit-the-inhibitors.
