In Search of the Brain's Language of Thought

Frankland & Greene · "Concepts and Compositionality" · Princeton / Harvard

MMaha Nawaz · October 13, 2026

Imagine Genghis Khan, Aretha Franklin and the Cleveland Cavaliers performing an opera on Maui. You've never thought that before, yet you just did, instantly. This review asks how the brain builds brand-new thoughts out of old parts.

The 60-second version
  1. The puzzle: thought is compositional. Finite parts, recombined, give infinite ideas.
  2. Fodor's answer (1975): the mind has a "language of thought" (LoT): symbols plus rules for combining them, separate from English and from perception.
  3. The authors' twist: the brain uses two complementary formats. Map-like codes in the default mode network (DMN) and sentence-like codes in temporal and prefrontal cortex.
  4. Maps (grid-cell-style, continuous) are great for similarity, interpolation and simulation, but bad at explicit relations like "A > B" or "A did X to B".
  5. Sentences (discrete role slots like agent/patient) handle who-did-what-to-whom and generalize to new combinations.
  6. Slogan: prefrontal "sentences" write the instructions; the DMN "map" is the canvas the thought is painted on.

Background: compositionality and the language of thought

Compositionality (Frege): the meaning of a whole is a function of the meanings of its parts and how they're combined. Language does this: a few thousand words plus syntax give infinitely many sentences. Fodor proposed thought itself works this way, in an internal, amodal (not tied to any sense or to any spoken language) symbolic code.

Two properties come for free with such a system:

ProductivityYou can think endlessly many new thoughts: "Abraham Lincoln serenaded by nineteen purple penguins on an aircraft carrier sailing down the Nile."
SystematicityIf you can think "the doctor needs an accountant", you can think "the accountant needs a doctor". Same parts, roles swapped.
The phrasebook contrast
A tourist's phrasebook has "Where is the train station?" and "Please take me to the hospital", but not "Please take me to the train station". Stored wholes don't recombine. The LoT says we are not phrasebook thinkers.
Try it: one relation, reusable parts
(,)
The structure relation(x, y) is stored once. Any concept can fill any slot, so 4 relations × 6 × 6 concepts already give 144 thoughts from 10 parts. Swapping x and y is systematicity. Notice "the dog chased the cat" and "the cat chased the dog" use identical parts but mean different things: meaning lives in the binding of concepts to roles.

The catch: Fodor's model was inspired by silicon computers with discrete symbols and addresses. Brains are "wet": noisy spikes, learned synapses. So how could neurons implement a symbol system? The review searches the neuroscience for candidate pieces.

Section · Conceptual combination

Evidence that the brain combines concepts

The basic experimental trick: compare a meaningful combination with a matched non-combination. Any region that responds more is a candidate "combiner".

Two examples worth slowing down for

Blue boat vs blue canoe. "Canoe" alone gets a bigger response than "boat" (it's more specific). But combined, blue boat beats blue canoe. Why? For "blue canoe" you just recolour a canoe. For "blue boat" you first have to pick what kind of boat (yacht? pirate ship?), then recolour it: a bigger trip through concept space.

Additive vs multiplicative combination (Baron & Osherson 2011). They asked how the brain pattern for old woman relates to the patterns for old and woman:

Toy 3-voxel patterns. Additive (old + woman): both parts stay recoverable side by side, like two memories retrieved together. Found in mPFC. Multiplicative (old × woman): voxels respond only where both parts are active, creating a new conjoint pattern, as in Smolensky's tensor-product binding. Found in mid-anterior STS and PCC.

Why both matter: compositionality needs independence ("doctor" means the same thing in both doctor sentences) and interaction (gray hair is like white hair, but gray clouds are like black clouds). Additive codes preserve the parts; multiplicative ones capture the interaction.

Section · Default mode network

The DMN as an amodal "canvas"

The DMN was first found as the set of regions more active at rest. Later it was tied to memory, imagining the future, navigation and theory of mind. Buckner's summary: it builds simulations of possible events. The review adds conceptual combination to the list and argues the DMN fits the LoT's first tenet: an amodal code.

MoviesWatching a TV episode evokes scene-specific DMN patterns, shared across people, which reappear when people later recall the scene aloud (Chen 2017).
Cross-modalHearing someone describe the scenes evokes the same DMN patterns as watching them (Zadbood 2017).
Words ⇄ picturesA classifier trained on the word "apple" can recognize a picture of an apple from DMN patterns (Fairhall & Caramazza 2013).
ContextDMN integrates over long time spans and its activity jumps when the task context switches.

Key nuance: the DMN probably doesn't store concept knowledge. That lives in domain-specific areas (e.g., tools vs animals). The DMN is a workspace that pulls knowledge in and arranges it for the current context. Hence "canvas", not "library".

Combining concepts is also more like inference than set intersection. A small car is bigger than a big grapefruit. To combine correctly you need background knowledge about how things usually are, which is the DMN's strength.

Section · Grid cells and conceptual maps

A navigation code reused for thinking

Place cells (hippocampus) fire at one location. Grid cells (entorhinal cortex) fire at many locations arranged in a repeating triangular lattice, like the centres of hexagons. Different modules use different spacings. Together they form an abstract coordinate system that's reused across environments.

One grid cell's firing fields as a rat explores a box. Each blob = a spot where the cell fires. Lines show the six-fold (60°) symmetry.
The fMRI trick (Doeller 2010). Moving along a grid axis (every 60°) gives a bit more signal than moving between axes. So BOLD vs direction has 6 bumps.
Try it: Constantinescu 2016 "bird space"
Participants watched a bird morph: neck and legs stretched or shrank at a chosen ratio. That ratio is a direction in an abstract 2-D "neck × legs" space. No real space is involved, yet mPFC, PCC and angular gyrus (DMN regions) showed the same 6-fold signal as spatial navigation. Grid alignment angle here is set to 15° for the demo.

Why this matters for combination: forming red boat = start at "boat", move along the colour dimension. Grid-like codes do exactly this vector displacement in navigation. Maps also support distance and "betweenness": a fax machine sits between a photocopier and a phone, which practically invites inventing it. Shepard's law (generalization falls off exponentially with psychological distance) suggests metric spaces are everywhere in cognition.

Neural networks trained just to predict where they'll be next spontaneously develop grid-like units (Whittington 2018, Cueva & Wei 2018, Banino 2018). And Webb et al. found that normalizing inputs to a learned map ("context normalization") lets networks extrapolate beyond their training range, as humans do.

Section · Limits of maps

What a grid code can't easily do

Fiete et al. (2008) showed grid codes are modulo codes, like a residue number system (RNS): a position is stored as its remainders after dividing by each module's period. This packs huge capacity into few cells and makes adding (moving) trivial. But it has a weakness.

Try it: residue code with moduli 3, 5, 7 (covers 0–104)

So a pure map struggles with explicit, directional relations: a > b, "A is left of B", "A did X to B". Decimal notation fixes this by adding positional syntax: you can see 222 > 52 from digit positions. That's a hint the brain also needs a sentence-like format.

Tversky's asymmetry
People say North Korea is more similar to China than China is to North Korea. In a true metric space, distance(A,B) = distance(B,A). Human similarity is directional, like a sentence with a subject and a reference point, not like a map.

And maps may only get you so far. You'd never wander into "Lincoln serenaded by penguins" on your own; reaching far-flung combinations may need explicit instructions from elsewhere.

Section · Who did what to whom

Finding sentence-like role slots in the brain

"The dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" contain the same concepts. Activating dog, man, bite isn't enough; the brain must bind each to a role. This is the authors' own research (Frankland & Greene 2015, 2019).

Schematic of the key result. Two neighbouring patches of left mid superior temporal cortex (lmSTC). A medial patch carries "who did it" (agent) and a lateral patch carries "to whom" (patient). The classifier was trained on some verbs and tested on new verbs, so the role code is reused across events.
Exp 1lmSTC patterns distinguish "the dog chased the cat" from "the cat chased the dog", in both active and passive voice. Sentences like "the baby kicked the grandfather" also modulate the amygdala via lmSTC.
Exp 2Separate agent and patient subregions. "Dog" in the patient region looks the same in "the cat chased the dog" and "the dog was bumped by the boy", despite different word order and verbs. That's reuse: a variable slot with a filler.
2019The roles follow the event's causal structure, not grammatical subject/object. And rostral prefrontal cortex (BA10) encodes narrow roles: dog-as-chaser vs dog-as-scratcher, which differ in posture and meaning.
PatientsDamage to mid-left STS/STG impairs understanding who did what to whom, for sentences and pictures ("the circle kissed the square").

Broad vs narrow roles

lmSTC · broad roles

agent = dog
Abstract, generalizes to any verb, maps onto sentence syntax. Like a symbol in Fodor's LoT.

rPFC · narrow roles

chaser = dog
Specific, carries how the relation plays out in the world. The authors call these "clip art": recombinable pieces with built-in detail.

Both regions sit between the flexible fronto-parietal control network and the DMN. The authors propose they're waypoints: compact "digital" codes that can generate, and be generated by, the rich "analog" scenes on the DMN canvas.

The proposed division of labour. Lateral prefrontal cortex runs the program; temporal and prefrontal waypoints hold discrete role bindings; the DMN renders the context-rich, continuous simulation.

Section · Computational models

How AI systems approach compositionality

GenerativeBayesian Program Learning (Lake 2015): learns a new handwritten character from one example by representing it as a composition of reusable strokes. β-VAEs learn factorized latent variables (one unit per factor, e.g. size or colour), useful building blocks. Recurrent generative models predicting next location grow grid-like codes.
Memory vs computeLike a Turing machine, separate what is stored from what operates on it. Kriete 2013's prefrontal–basal ganglia model uses pointers into role-specific slots and learns to put "John" in the agent slot after only seeing him as patient. The Differentiable Neural Computer has a controller network that reads and writes to an external memory matrix.
Cross-modalVisual question answering (CLEVR: "What size is the square left of the green metal thing right of the cylinder?") reaches 97–99.5%. Text-to-image models drew "a herd of elephants flying in blue skies" without ever seeing it. Both map vision and language into a shared space, an engineering cousin of an amodal LoT.
Authors' betUnconstrained deep nets learn whatever minimizes error. The authors think human-like generalization needs built-in variable binding and routing, and note that separating syntax and semantics in recurrent nets improves compositional generalization.
Indirection, the trick behind pointer models. Role slots don't hold the full concept; they hold an address pointing to it. Any concept can go in any slot without retraining the slot, so "John as agent" works even if John was only ever seen as a patient.

How it fits with the other two papers

Huth 2016Cited here as evidence for distributed semantic maps. Huth's semantic areas in parietal, medial and prefrontal cortex largely overlap the DMN, the regions this review calls the conceptual "canvas". Huth's maps capture what is talked about, a map-like format, but not role binding (word co-occurrence can't tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog").
Fedorenko 2024Strong agreement on the DMN: amodal, tracks narratives and situations over long time spans, not language-selective. A possible tension: Frankland & Greene's agent/patient region in left mid superior temporal cortex lies near or within the language network. Fedorenko would ask whether it's language-specific. This review notes the role effects also appear for pictures and videos, but admits people may silently translate them into language. Fedorenko's localizer approach would settle it.
TimelineThis review (2020) predates Fedorenko (2024), so it doesn't use her language/MD/ToM/DMN split.

Caveats the authors themselves stress

SpeculativeMuch of Fig 1b's mapping is dotted lines: interpretation, not proof.
DMN is pluralThe DMN is really several interleaved networks that group averages blur together.
Indirect gridHuman grid evidence is mostly the indirect fMRI 6-fold signal, not recordings of grid cells in prefrontal cortex.

Glossary

LoT
Language of thought: an internal symbolic code with reusable parts and combination rules.
Amodal
Not tied to one sense or input type.
Agent / patient
The doer / the one done-to in an event.
Binding
Linking a filler (dog) to a role (agent).
Tensor product
Binding by multiplying two vectors, giving a conjoint pattern.
fMRI adaptation
Repeat a representation → weaker response. Used to detect reuse.

Multi-voxel pattern analysis: decoding information from patterns rather than overall activation.
