The language network as a natural kind

Fedorenko, Ivanova & Regev · MIT / Georgia Tech

MMaha Nawaz · October 13, 2026

The claim: inside every adult brain there is a specific set of left frontal and temporal areas that does language, and only language. It's separate from the areas that hear sounds or move the mouth below it, and from the areas that think, reason and model other minds above it.

Natural kindBox 1Fig 1Fig 2Fig 3Box 2Fig 4Fig 5Try itvs Huth 2016Open questions
The 60-second version
  1. Find language areas in each person with a quick localizer (sentences > non-words), instead of averaging brains.
  2. Those areas respond to language in any form (spoken, written, signed, typed, any language) for both understanding and speaking.
  3. They barely respond to non-language: music, math, code, logic, pictures, gestures.
  4. All five areas behave almost identically. They care about words and how words combine, but not about how sentences link into a story.
  5. Below them: perception and motor areas that only care about the form (sounds, letter shapes, mouth movements), not meaning.
  6. Above them: reasoning networks (multiple demand, theory of mind, default mode) that care about content, whatever format it arrives in.

What "natural kind" means here

A natural kind is a grouping that reflects how nature is actually divided, not just a convenient label. "Mammal" is one; "things that are red" isn't. The authors argue the language network passes three tests:

ConnectedIts areas are wired together and their activity rises and falls together, even during rest or movies. You can find the network from correlations alone, with no language task.
CausalDamage to it causes aphasia (loss of language) while sparing math, music, reasoning and social understanding.
SelectiveIt responds to language and little else.
Box 1 · Method

Why you must localize in each person

This is the methodological backbone of the whole review. The same function sits in slightly different places in different brains. If you average brains voxel by voxel in a common template, the blobs smear together, and neighbouring areas with different jobs blend into one.

Toy illustration along one strip of cortex. Each person has a sharp language area (purple) next to a speech-perception area (blue), but in slightly different spots. The group average (bottom) shows one broad, weak smear where the two areas overlap, so you can't tell them apart.

The fix is functional localization: run a short, well-tested contrast in every person, define their areas from it, then measure those exact areas on the experiment you care about. For language, the standard localizer is:

The language localizer contrast
Sentences: "NOBODY COULD HAVE PREDICTED THE EARTHQUAKE"
>
Non-word lists: "U BIZBY ACWORRILY MAPE LAS POME"
Both look and sound alike, but only one has words and meaning. Areas that respond more to the first are the language network. Alternatives (intact vs degraded speech, native vs unfamiliar language) find the same areas.

Two more rules from Box 1: independent data must be used to define the areas and to measure them (otherwise the result is circular), and "Broca's area" defined by anatomy covers several functionally different areas, which has caused decades of confusion.

Box 2 · Philosophy

"Language network" is a label, not a definition

Our words for mental functions (language, syntax, reasoning) came from folk psychology and linguistics, not from brains. The brain may not split things the same way. Here the match is good but not perfect: discourse is part of "language" as linguists define it, yet the language network doesn't handle it. And syntax vs semantics, a core distinction in linguistics, doesn't map onto separate brain areas. So treat the label as a shorthand that may need revising.

Figure 4 · The neighbours below

Form areas vs meaning areas

Take the non-word "blork". You can hear it, repeat it, read it and write it, but it means nothing. Perception and motor areas handle it fine. The language network barely cares. Fig 4b shows this with four stimulus types across five kinds of area.

Fig 4b, redrawn. Bars: sentences, word lists, non-word lists, non-linguistic stimuli. Only the language network shows a staircase: it tracks how much meaning and structure there is.
PrimaryA1, V1, motor cortex: respond to everything that hits the ears, eyes or muscles. Not language-specific at all.
PerceptionSpeech perception area and VWFA: high for anything language-shaped, including non-words, low for other sounds or images. Selective for form.
LanguageStaircase: sentences > word lists > non-word lists > non-linguistic. Selective for meaning and structure.
Motor planBroca's area (articulation) and putative Exner's area (writing): high for producing any speech or writing, including nonsense syllables.

Fig 5, redrawn. Purple bars = language network; green bars = the matching reasoning network. Language goes high for anything verbal (1, 2) and drops for the non-verbal version (3). Each reasoning network ignores plain language (1) but responds to its content in either format (2 and 3).
Multiple demandBilateral frontal and parietal. Hard tasks of any kind: working memory, math, logic, novel problems. Linked to fluid intelligence. Recruited for language only when there's an extra task (answer a question) or effortful listening (noisy, accented, foreign speech).
Theory of mindTemporo-parietal junction plus midline areas. Thinking about beliefs and intentions. Needed for sarcasm, indirect requests ("it's cold in here" = close the window), and conversations.
Default modeMedial prefrontal, medial parietal, TPJ, temporal pole. Tracks context over minutes (vs a few words for the language network). Builds the situation model of a story. Same response to a story and a movie of the same events.
Why this is a double dissociation
Area A responds to X but not Y; area B responds to Y but not X. That's stronger than a single difference, because neither area can just be "less active overall". Patients show the same split: people with severe aphasia can still do arithmetic, play chess and understand others' minds.

How this connects to Huth 2016

The review cites Huth et al. (ref. 20) and the two papers fit together in an interesting way.

AgreementBoth find left frontal and temporal regions involved in language meaning. The review says naturalistic listening "engages a broad set of brain areas beyond the language network", citing Huth.
ReframingMany of Huth's semantic areas (LPC, MPC, superior prefrontal) are default mode and theory-of-mind territory. Huth himself noted they overlap the default mode network. On the review's view, those maps show the reasoning and situation-model networks tracking story content, not the language network.
BilateralityHuth found surprisingly symmetric maps. The review's language network is left-lateralized, but the default mode and theory-of-mind networks are bilateral. Mixing those together in a story would naturally look bilateral.
MethodHuth's voxel-wise models are fit in each person but the semantic areas weren't defined with a language localizer. Without one, you can't say which network a semantic voxel belongs to (Box 1).
Angular gyrusHuth's social-concept "bullseye" in lateral parietal cortex is around the angular gyrus, the very area this review removed from the language network.

Open questions the authors flag

MeaningThe network responds to meaningful language, not meaning in general (pictures barely drive it). It even likes "Colourless green ideas sleep furiously". So they propose two steps: the language network pulls word meanings and combines them by rule; other networks turn that into deep, world-aware understanding.
InterfacesWhat format does information take when it passes from the language network to the reasoning networks, and how do they influence each other in real time?
EdgesThe blobs are drawn with sharp edges, but borders might be gradients. That wouldn't undo the separation of the peaks.
MechanismIntracranial recordings and neural network language models (which predict language-network responses well) should reveal the actual computations.
DevelopmentSpeech perception and articulation develop before the language network, which develops before reasoning. Tracking that could clarify how they come apart.
ThoughtIf aphasia leaves reasoning intact, language isn't required for thought, though labels may still help some tasks (working memory, counting, learning concepts).

Glossary

Localizer
A short, validated contrast used to find an area in each individual.
fROI
Functional region of interest: one person's area, defined by the localizer.
Parcel
A broad search zone from the group map, in which each person's fROI is looked for.
Selectivity
Responding much more to one kind of input than to others.
Modality-independent
Same response whether input is spoken, written or signed.
Jabberwocky
Grammatical sentences with made-up content words.
Aphasia
Loss of language ability after brain damage.
Apraxia / dysarthria
Speech-planning deficit (Broca's area) / slurred execution (motor cortex).
Receptive window
How much past input an area integrates: ~500 ms (speech), a few words (language), minutes (default mode).
