Shape perception from 1,024 electrodes in monkey visual cortex, explained figure by figure

Chen, Wang, Fernández & Roelfsema · Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience · vol. 370, pp. 1191–1196 · doi:10.1126/science.abd7435

MMaha Nawaz · October 14, 2026

Zap one spot in the visual cortex and you see a dot of light, a phosphene. People have known that since the 1960s. This paper asks the next question, the one any visual prosthesis depends on: if you zap many spots at once, does the brain combine the dots into a shape? Two monkeys with over a thousand implanted electrodes answered yes. They recognized lines, motion and letters made purely from stimulation, on the very first try.

Before the figures

Five things you need to know

1. V1 is a map of what you see. The primary visual cortex (V1) at the back of the brain is laid out like a screen. Neighbouring points of cortex respond to neighbouring points in the visual field. This is called retinotopy. The map is flipped: the left hemisphere handles the right half of vision, and the top of V1 handles the lower half. That is why every dot in this paper shows up in the lower-right of the monkey's view.

2. Every electrode "owns" one spot. The spot in the visual field that makes a neuron fire is its receptive field (RF). Measure the RF under each electrode and you have a lookup table: electrode #417 ↔ 3° right, 2° down.

3. Stimulate an electrode and a dot appears at its RF. That dot is a phosphene. Phosphenes from V1 are mostly small white or grey dots, sometimes coloured. They range from a pinpoint to about 2 cm across at arm's length.

4. V4 sits downstream of V1. V4 is a higher visual area that receives V1's output and handles shape. If stimulation in V1 is strong enough to be "seen", the signal should show up in V4. The authors use this as a built-in meter for perception.

5. Monkeys answer with their eyes. They can't talk, so every task ends with a saccade, a fast eye movement to a target. An eye tracker records where they looked. "Look where the dot was", "look up for vertical", "look at the T-target if you saw a T". Correct answers earn juice.

The bet. Earlier human work used electrodes on the brain's surface. These need milliamps, light up millimetres of cortex, and blur into big blobs. This paper uses electrodes that penetrate the cortex. They need about 100× less current, activate a few hundred µm of tissue, and give sharp, small dots. With enough of them you get a dot-matrix display.

How RFs were measured. Bars of light swept across a screen while the authors recorded each electrode. The moment an electrode fired tells you where its RF is. Movies S1 and S2 in the paper show this.

Takeaway. Each electrode works as a pixel at a known address. The monkey sees the dot in the right direction, but reports it a bit closer to center than the RF predicts.
Figure 2

Saccade-to-phosphene: can the monkey see one dot, and how much current does it take?

Figure 2 has three jobs. It measures the threshold current for each electrode (B, C). It shows that V4 activity predicts that threshold without asking the monkey anything (D–H). And it checks that the dot appears in the right place (I, J).

Panel A · the task

For comparison, surface electrodes in earlier human work needed milliamps, roughly 1,000+ µA. Small currents mean small activated volumes, which means sharp dots, and many electrodes can run at once without the fields merging.

Panels D, G, H · V4 as a built-in perception meter

  • D
    The setup. Stimulate in V1, record in V4. The stimulation creates a huge electrical artifact in the recording, which the authors removed before measuring the real neural response.
  • G
    The key panel. One dot per electrode: V4-based threshold (R₅₀, x) vs behaviour-based threshold (C₅₀, y). They fall on a straight line. r = 0.98 in monkey L (169 electrodes) and r = 0.96 in monkey A (64 electrodes).
  • H
    Split electrodes into three groups by threshold: low (blue), medium (red), high (yellow). Top row is behaviour, bottom row is V4. The curves have the same shape and the same left-to-right order. So V4 tracks perception across the whole curve, not just at the 50% point.
Calibrating 1,000 electrodes by asking a blind patient "did you see it?" 50 times each is 50,000 questions. That's days of tedious testing. Panel G says you can instead watch a downstream brain area "light up" and read the threshold off it, potentially while the person is doing something else. It's like tuning a radio by watching the signal meter instead of asking a listener.

Panels I, J · is the dot in the right place?

  • I
    Direction (polar angle). Saccade angle vs RF angle. r = 0.77 (L) and 0.67 (A). The dots point the right way.
  • J
    Distance (eccentricity). Saccade distance vs RF distance. r = 0.90 (L) and 0.72 (A). The blue fit line is flatter than the dashed 1:1 line: the eyes landed at 77 ± 18% (L) and 71 ± 21% (A) of the way to the RF. The undershoot was larger for weak currents.
Takeaway. Single electrodes reliably make visible dots at 10s of µA, in the right place. V4 recordings predict each electrode's threshold almost perfectly, which removes the main obstacle to calibrating a thousand-channel implant.
Figure 3

Two dots and a moving dot: the first composite percepts

Now the real question. Do several phosphenes at once combine into something with structure? The simplest structure is two dots: are they side by side or stacked? The simplest change over time is motion: which way did the dot move?

Why the "first trial" column matters most

Takeaway. Simultaneous stimulation produces spatial layout (which way two dots line up). Stimulation in sequence produces motion. The monkeys transferred what they'd learned with real dots straight to the stimulation version, so the percepts must look like real dots arranged in space.

The control logic (panel D, grey)

A skeptic would say the monkeys simply learned "pattern X → left target" by trial and error. The authors tested this directly, twice.

  • 1
    Random electrode sets. Non-overlapping groups whose RFs formed no letter (10 sets per monkey). The monkeys had to learn arbitrary pattern → target links. After 100 trials they were still at chance: 48.2% and 46.9%.
  • 2
    Unfamiliar visual dot patterns. The same idea with real dots on screen: 50.5% and 49.9%. Still chance.

So these monkeys can't learn an arbitrary mapping in a few trials. When they get letters right by trial 3–7, the only explanation left is that the stimulated pattern looked like the letter they already knew.

Takeaway. Firing 8–15 V1 electrodes at once produces a recognizable letter. That is the first demonstration of real shape vision from simultaneous intracortical stimulation, and the core proof of concept for a cortical visual prosthesis.
Discussion

Why it worked, and what's still missing

Three ingredients

  • 1
    Lots of electrodes over a continuous region. Simulations of phosphene vision suggest a few hundred electrodes are enough to read letters, while fluent reading and finding objects in clutter may need thousands. 1,000+ over one quadrant was enough for simple shapes.
  • 2
    Penetrating electrodes. Thresholds of 23–50 µA vs milliamps for surface electrodes. Each one activates neurons within a few hundred µm of the tip, giving smaller, sharper dots. A direct comparison of surface vs penetrating phosphenes in the same subject hasn't been done yet.
  • 3
    Training with dot shapes beforehand. The monkeys already knew what sparse dot letters look like, so they recognized stimulation letters immediately. A blind patient would need an equivalent rehabilitation program.

Limitations the authors list

  • a
    Stimulation was worse than real vision, and some electrode sets were near chance. Individual phosphenes vary in size, brightness, colour and apparent depth, and you can't control those yet.
  • b
    Dots merge. Two electrodes a few hundred µm apart can fuse into one phosphene. This caps the useful pixel density.
  • c
    Scale. Human V1 is about 25–30 cm² per hemisphere. A useful implant must cover far more of it with enough density.
  • d
    Engineering. Needs wireless high-channel-count links and long-lasting, biocompatible electrodes that minimize scarring (gliosis), tissue trauma and encapsulation.

Conflict of interest: Roelfsema and Chen co-founded Phosphoenix, a visual-prosthesis startup, and filed a related patent. Coauthor Eduardo Fernández led the 2021 human study with a 96-channel array in a blind volunteer's visual cortex.

Connection

How this maps onto Blindsight

Blindsight is the same idea as this paper: a camera feeds a processor, which stimulates electrodes in visual cortex to make phosphene images. The differences are in hardware and patients.

Questions this paper left me with

  1. Automatic calibration. Do they read thresholds from recorded brain activity, like the V4 trick, instead of asking the patient about every electrode?
  2. Phosphene merging. At their thread spacing, how close can two stimulation sites get before the dots fuse?
  3. Where the dots land. How do they correct for the undershoot and for mismatches between an electrode's RF and where the person actually sees the dot?
  4. Rehabilitation. These monkeys trained for months on dot letters first. What is the plan for people blind since birth, whose V1 has never had normal visual input?
  5. Long-term stability. Utah array thresholds drift as scar tissue builds. Do flexible threads keep thresholds stable over years?

Reference

Glossary

Phosphene
A dot of light seen without light entering the eye, here caused by stimulating visual cortex.
V1 / V4
Primary visual cortex, the first cortical stop for visual input and a fine-grained map of the visual field; and a higher visual area downstream that handles shape and colour.
Receptive field (RF)
The patch of the visual field that drives a given neuron or electrode.
Retinotopy
The orderly mapping of neighbouring visual-field points onto neighbouring cortex.
Eccentricity / polar angle
Polar coordinates for the visual field: distance from the center of gaze (degrees) and direction around it.
Saccade
A fast eye movement from one point to another. The monkeys' "answer".
C₅₀ / R₅₀
Current at which behaviour reaches 50% detection (C₅₀), or V4 activity reaches 50% of its maximum (R₅₀).
Psychometric / neurometric curve
Performance vs stimulus strength, measured from behaviour or from neural activity.
MUA
Multi-unit activity: the combined spiking of many neurons near an electrode tip.
Utah array
A 4×4 mm silicon bed of needle electrodes (here 8×8, 1.5 mm long), the standard human research implant.
Critical trial (c)
The first trial number at which accuracy across electrode sets is significantly above chance (binomial test).
