Reading the PAM

Four things the PAM-recognition section assumes you already know: which strand is which, what actually comes apart, how two arginines read two guanines, and why one cut needs two nucleases.

Reading notes on Chen & Doudna, Nat. Rev. Chem. 1, 0078 (2017), pp. 5–7 & Fig. 3a

The naming

Target strand and non-target strand

The names are assigned from the guide RNA's point of view, not the protein's. That is the whole trick, and nothing in the section makes sense until it lands.

  • The target strand is the one the guide RNA pairs with. It is complementary to the guide.
  • The non-target strand is the other one. It has the same sequence as the guide RNA (T where the RNA has U), so the guide cannot pair with it — it gets pushed out of the way.

A trap in the name. "Non-target" does not mean unimportant or ignored. That strand carries the PAM, gets threaded into its own tunnel in the protein, and is cut. It is only "non-target" in the narrow sense that the guide RNA does not bind it.

My first question

Is the NGG unzipped?

No. The PAM stays double-stranded the entire time. This is the point the paper makes by calling it "the PAM duplex" — the word duplex is telling you it is still paired.

Cas9 reads the two Gs by reaching into the major groove of intact double-stranded DNA. The edges of base pairs are exposed in that groove, so a protein can identify a sequence without opening it. Only the 20 bp next to the PAM comes apart.

Four steps, in order

The phosphate lock-loop

First: what "+1" means

Positions are counted outward from the boundary between the PAM and the protospacer, and the numbers refer to phosphates in the backbone, not bases.

  • + counts into the target sequence — +1 is the first phosphate inside the protospacer, right at the boundary
  • − counts the other way, into the PAM
  • * means the position is on the non-target strand

Then: why the DNA bends at all

Nothing pushes it. The binding energy pays for the bend. Bending DNA is uphill on its own, but Cas9's contacts — the positive groove on the backbone, the two arginines on the GG, the lock-loop on the +1 phosphate — release far more energy than the bend costs, so the bent state is the favourable one overall.

Geometrically: two points on the DNA get pinned to fixed positions on a rigid protein. The β-hairpin holds the PAM duplex; the lock-loop holds the +1 phosphate. The stretch of DNA between those two grips is longer than the straight-line distance between them, so it has nowhere to go but to bend. Cas9's surface is shaped to fit bent DNA — the DNA deforms to fit the mould.

There is no ATP anywhere in this. The paper says so directly: "dsDNA unwinding by Cas9 is ATP independent." No motor, no fuel. Every bit of the deformation is paid for out of binding free energy — the same trick enzymes use generally, spending binding energy to strain a substrate toward the state you want it in.

The four steps below are a real temporal sequence, so they are numbered. Watch what the DNA does, not the protein — the protein stays rigid and the DNA bends, which is the surprise the paper flags as "induced fit".

STEP 1

Collide and grip

Random three-dimensional collision. A positively charged groove grabs the negatively charged backbone. Sequence-blind so far — it will grab anything.

STEP 2

Read the GG

Two arginines reach into the major groove and hydrogen-bond to the guanine edges. No unzipping — the sequence is read through intact DNA.

STEP 3

Kink, then lock

Two protrusions, two jobs. The β-hairpin is still holding the GG from step 2 — it does not move. The lock-loop (Lys1107, Ser1109) is a floppy connecting segment that swings in and clamps the +1 phosphate, holding the DNA's new bend in place.

STEP 4

A window opens

The bend strains the neighbouring base pairs and flips three bases out into solvent. Those exposed bases are the nucleation site — the foothold the guide RNA tests first.

What "flips three bases out into the solvent" means

In an intact duplex every base is doing two jobs at once: paired across to its partner, and stacked face-to-face with the bases above and below it. Both jobs require the base to sit inside the helix. Nothing is exposed except the edges facing into the grooves.

Flipping out means a base rotates about its backbone bonds and swings clear of the stack, so it points into the surrounding water — the "bulk solvent". It stays attached to the backbone; it just stops being part of the helix.

Why it happens here: a sharp kink wrecks the local stacking geometry. Once the backbone turns a corner, the bases on either side of the corner can no longer sit flat against each other, and rotating out relieves the strain. Cas9 also provides surfaces that hold the flipped-out bases in place.

What it results in: three base pairs are now broken, which opens a short single-stranded window at the PAM-proximal edge of the protospacer. In an intact duplex the target strand's bases are already occupied by their partners — the guide RNA has nothing to bind. Break three pairs and the RNA finally has somewhere to start.

That window is the toehold. Once the guide gets two or three base pairs into it, each new RNA–DNA pair releases enough energy to break the next DNA–DNA pair, and the hybrid zips along away from the PAM. This is why R-loop formation is unidirectional: it can only start where the window is.

Base flipping is a general enzymatic move, not a Cas9 peculiarity. DNA methyltransferases and repair glycosylases flip their target base fully out of the helix and into an active-site pocket, because chemistry on a base buried in a stack is impossible. Cas9 uses the same manoeuvre for a different purpose — not to react with the base, but to make it available for pairing.

The authors then undercut this. Mutating the lock-loop residues did not reduce cleavage, and the loop is not conserved across Cas9s from other species. A 2017 study proposed it helps the guide RNA pair rather than helps the DNA melt. Steps 3 and 4 above are the leading model, not settled fact — which is why the paragraph is full of "suggested", "proposed" and "however".

The chemistry

Why swapping in an arginine lets you read a guanine

Guanine's edge, where it faces into the major groove, presents two hydrogen-bond acceptors — the O6 oxygen and the N7 nitrogen — sitting a fixed distance apart. Any side chain that wants to identify a G has to place two hydrogen-bond donors at that same spacing.

Arginine does exactly that. Its guanidinium group is a flat fork with two N–H donors, geometrically matched to O6 and N7. Two bonds at once — a bidentate contact — which is both strong and specific. Arginine is the standard guanine reader across all of biology, not just in Cas9.

Threonine cannot. One short side chain, one hydroxyl, one possible bond, and too little reach. It can touch DNA, but it cannot say "this one is a G."

Why this is the paragraph that matters for design work. PAM specificity — the hard limit on where CRISPR can be aimed — comes down to a handful of side chains reaching into a groove. That is a tractable design problem: identify which positions contact which bases, then swap in side chains whose donor/acceptor geometry matches the base you want read.

Two strands, two enzymes

HNH and RuvC

Cas9 has two separate nuclease active sites — two distinct pockets, in two distinct domains, using two different chemistries. Each is responsible for one strand.

DomainCutsMechanism
HNH
D839 · H840 · N863		 target strand
the one paired with the guide RNA		 one Mg²⁺; His840 activates a water molecule
RuvC
D10 · E762 · H983 · D986		 non-target strand

Neither cuts both strands. A double-strand break requires both firing in a coordinated way — which is why the paper spends a whole section on allosteric activation: something has to tell HNH it is safe to cut, and HNH in turn signals RuvC.
